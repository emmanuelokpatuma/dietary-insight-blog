HbA1c Levels Explained: What Your Result Means in the UK

HbA1c levels explained: what the test measures, the NHS ranges in mmol/mol and %, how often to have it and what can change your result. A plain UK guide.

What are HbA1c levels? HbA1c is a blood test that shows your average blood glucose (sugar) level over the last 2 to 3 months. The NHS says a result below 42mmol/mol (6%) is normal, 42 to 47mmol/mol (6% to 6.5%) may mean you’re at risk of diabetes, and 48mmol/mol (6.5%) or above may mean you have diabetes.

What does HbA1c actually measure?

Diabetes UK explains that HbA1c is glycated haemoglobin. It forms when glucose in your blood sticks to your red blood cells.

Red blood cells are active for around 2 to 3 months, which is why the result reflects your average blood sugar over that time. That’s different from a finger-prick test, which Diabetes UK describes as a snapshot of your blood sugar at one moment.

The NHS uses the HbA1c test both to diagnose type 2 diabetes and to monitor type 1 or type 2 diabetes once it has been diagnosed.

What do HbA1c results mean?

Results are usually given in mmol/mol (millimoles per mole), but the NHS says they’re sometimes given as a percentage. Here’s how the NHS describes the ranges:

HbA1c result If you do not have diabetes If you have diabetes
Below 42mmol/mol (6%) Normal No separate wording given
42 to 47mmol/mol (6% to 6.5%) You may be at risk of getting diabetes (sometimes called prediabetes) Your diabetes is well controlled
48mmol/mol (6.5%) or above You may have diabetes Your doctor may review your treatment

Below 42mmol/mol (6%) (if you do not have diabetes Normal): no separate wording given

42 to 47mmol/mol (6% to 6.5%) (if you do not have diabetes You may be at risk of getting diabetes (sometimes called prediabetes)): your diabetes is well controlled

48mmol/mol (6.5%) or above (if you do not have diabetes You may have diabetes): your doctor may review your treatment

The NHS stresses that these levels are a guide. You may have agreed a different target with your care team, and results can be affected by other conditions or medicines.

If you’re in the at-risk range, our guide to what prediabetes is explains more.

What is a good HbA1c target if you have diabetes?

Diabetes UK says that for people with diabetes, an ideal HbA1c is 48mmol/mol (6.5%) or below. For people at risk of type 2 diabetes, it gives a target of below 42mmol/mol (6%).

Your target is personal, though. Diabetes UK says your healthcare team may set an individual target for you, and may aim to bring your level down in stages. For some older people, targets may be set higher because of the risk of a fall linked to low blood sugar.

The NHS notes that if diabetes isn’t well controlled, it can raise the risk of problems such as eye, kidney and nerve problems. That’s why it’s worth knowing your number and talking it through with your team.

How is the HbA1c test done?

The NHS describes it as a standard blood test:

  1. You don’t need to prepare. You can eat and drink as normal beforehand.
  2. A healthcare professional takes a small amount of blood, usually from the inside of your elbow.
  3. You may get a little bruising or swelling, which should go away after a few days.
  4. Results usually come back after a few days. If you haven’t heard anything after a few weeks, contact the GP surgery or specialist who arranged the test.

You may also be able to see your results in the NHS App.

How often should you have an HbA1c test?

The NHS says people with diabetes are usually advised to have an HbA1c test every 3 to 6 months. Diabetes UK says you’re entitled to one at least once a year.

Diabetes UK adds that some people need it more often, for example if they’re planning a baby, changing treatment or having problems managing their levels. If you haven’t had an HbA1c test in over a year, it says to contact your healthcare team.

Writing your results down, for example in a diary, can help you spot trends over time, Diabetes UK suggests.

What can affect your HbA1c result?

Diabetes UK lists several things that can change your HbA1c:

  • being unwell
  • some medicines, such as steroids
  • changes to your lifestyle
  • feeling very stressed or depressed

Some conditions can also make the result less accurate, including haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell anaemia and thalassaemia. If any of these apply to you, Diabetes UK suggests asking your doctor whether a different test would be more accurate.

What happens if your HbA1c is high?

If you haven’t been diagnosed with diabetes, the NHS says you may need another test to confirm the result. Either way, it says you may be advised to make lifestyle changes, such as getting more exercise, losing weight and changing your diet, and you may be offered medicine or changes to your current treatment.

Diabetes UK’s suggestions for bringing HbA1c down include:

  1. Getting active. It says exercise can specifically help to bring down HbA1c levels.
  2. Eating a healthy, balanced diet.
  3. Stopping smoking, as smoking makes it harder for blood to flow around your body.
  4. Going on a local diabetes education course.
  5. Reviewing your treatment with your healthcare team.

Don’t change any medicine or insulin on your own. Talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian about what your result means and what to do next. If you’re starting with food, our low GI foods list is a practical place to begin.

Your HbA1c reflects your blood sugar over 2 to 3 months, and Diabetes UK lists a healthy, balanced diet among the ways to help bring it down. Frequently asked questions

Do I need to fast before an HbA1c test?

No. The NHS says you do not need to do anything to prepare for an HbA1c test, and you can eat and drink as normal.

What HbA1c level means diabetes?

The NHS says a result of 48mmol/mol (6.5%) or above means you may have diabetes. If you have not been diagnosed before, you may be asked to have another test to confirm it.

How often should I have an HbA1c test?

Diabetes UK says you're entitled to an HbA1c test at least once a year, and the NHS says people with diabetes are usually advised to have one every 3 to 6 months. If you haven't had one for over a year, Diabetes UK says to contact your healthcare team.
