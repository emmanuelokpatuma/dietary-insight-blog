Is Pasta Good for Diabetics? Portions, Wholewheat and GI Tips

Pasta can fit into a diabetes diet. UK guidance on portion sizes, wholewheat swaps, GI, cooled pasta and lighter sauces to keep blood sugar steadier.

Is pasta good for diabetics? Pasta can be part of a healthy diet when you have diabetes. The key things are the portion size, choosing wholewheat where you can and keeping an eye on the sauce, because the total amount of carbohydrate you eat has the biggest effect on your blood sugar after a meal.

Can you eat pasta if you have diabetes?

Yes. Diabetes UK lists pasta alongside bread, rice, potatoes and plantain as a starchy carbohydrate food. It also says not everyone with diabetes needs to restrict their carb intake.

The NHS recommends that starchy carbohydrates make up just over a third of the food you eat, and suggests including at least one starchy food with each main meal. Pasta is one of the options it names.

What matters most is how much you have and what you have it with.

How much pasta is a portion?

Diabetes UK says the total amount of carbohydrate you eat has the biggest effect on your glucose levels after eating. So portion size is the first thing to look at.

Its guide portion sizes for some common starchy foods are:

Food Diabetes UK guide portion
Boiled pasta or cooked noodles About what fits in two hands cupped together (180g)
Cooked rice About what fits in two hands cupped together (180g)
Jacket potato About the size of your fist (220g)
Bread 1 medium slice (35g)

Diabetes UK says these portions are a guide based on an average healthy adult, and your dietitian can advise on the portions that are right for you. It also says not to assume the portion sizes listed on food packaging are right for you.

Pasta can be tricky to measure at first. Diabetes UK suggests using the same container each time to measure out foods like pasta and rice, or weighing your food if you want to be accurate.

Is wholewheat pasta better than white pasta?

The NHS recommends higher fibre or wholegrain varieties, such as wholewheat pasta, because they contain more fibre than white or refined starchy carbohydrates and can help you feel full for longer.

According to Diabetes UK, wholegrain foods are usually better for managing blood glucose levels because they tend to have a lower glycaemic index (GI). It also says that adults should be aiming for 30g of fibre a day, and many of us don’t get that much.

There is one catch. Diabetes UK points out that wholegrains are still carbohydrate foods, and all carbohydrates affect blood glucose levels, so it’s still worth being mindful of your portion size.

What is the GI of pasta, and does it matter?

The glycaemic index ranks carbohydrate foods from 0 to 100 by how quickly they raise blood sugar. Diabetes UK says a GI of 55 or below raises blood sugar slowly, 56 to 69 at a medium rate, and 70 or more quickly. Our low GI foods list explains this in more detail.

Diabetes UK says GI doesn’t take portion size into account, and that the amount of carbs you eat has a bigger effect on blood sugar than GI alone. It gives pasta as an example: pasta has a lower GI than watermelon, but it has more carbs, so if you eat similar amounts of both, the pasta will have more of an impact on your blood sugar.

Its advice is to watch your carbohydrate portion sizes first, then choose lower-GI options as an added bonus. One of the swaps it suggests is wholewheat pasta cooked al dente.

Does cooling pasta make a difference?

Diabetes UK explains that glucose from cooked starchy foods such as white rice, pasta and potatoes is absorbed almost as quickly as glucose from a sugary drink. When starchy foods are cooled, their structure changes and the enzymes in your gut can’t break them down as easily. This is called resistant starch.

Diabetes UK says there is a small amount of research suggesting that reheating cooled starchy foods creates more resistant starch. One swap it suggests is cooked and cooled pasta in a pasta salad instead of hot pasta with sauce.

Which pasta sauces are the better choice?

The sauce can change a pasta dish a lot. The NHS says it’s the fats you add when cooking or serving starchy foods that increase the calorie content, and gives creamy sauces on pasta as an example.

Diabetes UK says protein, fat and fibre slow down the digestion and absorption of carbohydrates, and foods containing them can help you feel fuller for longer and keep blood sugar levels more stable.

A few practical ideas based on that guidance:

  1. Fill up on vegetables. Diabetes UK suggests piling your plate with vegetables, as they’re naturally low in calories and high in fibre.
  2. Add some protein. Diabetes UK says protein lowers the GI of a food, so try adding beans, lentils, chicken or fish.
  3. Go lighter on creamy sauces. Use them as an occasional choice rather than every time.
  4. Check the salt in jars. The NHS says about three-quarters of the salt we eat is already in food when we buy it, including sauces.

How can I keep my blood sugar steadier after pasta?

Diabetes UK says spreading carbs evenly throughout the day and avoiding large portions all at once can help keep sugar levels stable between meals. It also suggests eating slowly, as it takes about 20 minutes for your brain to register that you’re full.

Diabetes UK also says eating more carbohydrate than usual can cause blood glucose to go too high, and eating less than usual can cause a hypo. Talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian about how pasta fits into your meals and medication. If you’d like to learn more about using GI, Diabetes UK suggests asking your GP about a referral to a registered dietitian.

Frequently asked questions

How much pasta should I eat if I have diabetes?

Diabetes UK gives a guide portion of boiled pasta as about the amount that would fit in two hands cupped together (180g). It says this is a guide for an average healthy adult, and your dietitian can advise on portions that are right for you.

Is wholewheat pasta better than white pasta for diabetes?

Diabetes UK says wholegrain foods are usually better for managing blood glucose because they tend to have a lower GI, and it suggests wholewheat pasta cooked al dente as a swap. They are still carbohydrates, so portion size still matters.

Does cooling pasta change how it affects blood sugar?

Diabetes UK says that when starchy foods are cooled, their structure changes and gut enzymes can't break them down as easily. It suggests cooked and cooled pasta in a pasta salad as a swap for hot pasta with sauce.
