Healthy Takeaway Options for Diabetes: A UK Menu Guide

Healthy takeaway options for diabetes: what to order from the curry house, chippy, pizza place, Chinese, Thai and kebab shop, with simple UK swaps.

Healthy takeaway options for diabetes usually come down to three things: how the food is cooked, what you have on the side and how big the portion is. Grilled, baked, steamed and tomato-based dishes are generally better choices than fried or creamy ones, and swapping fried rice, naan or a large portion of chips for plainer sides can help too.

Can you eat takeaways with diabetes?

Yes. Diabetes UK says having diabetes shouldn’t stop you enjoying eating out. Its tips are about choosing portion sizes, toppings and sides carefully.

It helps to know where the fat and salt hide. The British Heart Foundation (BHF) says most shop-bought or restaurant pizzas are high in saturated fat and can be salty too, and that a chicken korma is likely to be high in saturated fat and potentially salt.

The NHS says the average man should eat no more than 30g of saturated fat a day and the average woman no more than 20g, so it is worth knowing which dishes are richest.

How to order a healthier Indian takeaway

Diabetes UK suggests tandoori and tikka options because they are baked and lower in fat. The NHS also recommends dry or tomato-based dishes, such as tandoori or madras, instead of creamy curries like korma, pasanda or masala.

For sides, choose boiled or steamed rice rather than pilau or fried rice, and wholemeal chapatti or roti rather than naan. Diabetes UK points out that naans are often very large, made with low-fibre white flour and may have a lot of butter.

Diabetes UK has a simple plate tip: fill your plate with salad and a portion of unsweetened yoghurt first, then add rice to the space that’s left. Dhal is rich in fibre but can be quite oily, so sharing a portion works well. Our guide is rice bad for diabetes has more on rice portions.

What about Chinese and Thai takeaways?

The NHS suggests lower-fat Chinese dishes such as steamed fish, chicken chop suey or szechuan prawns. Diabetes UK recommends broth-based soups or a Thai salad as a starter rather than spring rolls or satays, with dressings and dips served on the side.

Diabetes UK also suggests steamed or fragrant basmati rice rather than fried rice.

For Thai food, the NHS says to try stir-fried or steamed dishes with chicken, fish or vegetables. Curries made with coconut milk are high in saturated fat, so if you choose one, try not to eat all the sauce.

Fish and chips, pizza and burgers

Fish and chips: Diabetes UK suggests ordering a smaller portion and removing the batter from the fish. Go for thick-cut chips, because the thicker the chip, the less fat it absorbs. The BHF adds that you can ask them to hold the salt, mushy peas add fibre, and some chip shops will grill your fish if you ask.

Pizza: Diabetes UK recommends a thin base, sharing a pizza and filling up on a side salad. The BHF says cheese and meaty toppings like salami, pepperoni, ham and bacon are the main sources of saturated fat and salt, and the NHS suggests lower-fat toppings such as vegetables, chicken, tuna or other seafood instead.

Burgers: try the lean or veggie option and leave out the cheese and mayonnaise. Diabetes UK says going bun-less is a lower carb option, and you can ask for extra lettuce, tomatoes and gherkins, though it’s worth watching the dressing.

Kebabs: the NHS suggests a shish kebab with pitta bread and salad rather than a doner.

Simple habits that help with any takeaway

  1. Check the calories. Diabetes UK says restaurants, cafes and takeaways that employ more than 250 people have to show calories on their menus, which makes comparing dishes easier.
  2. Watch the extras. Diabetes UK says to watch out for extras such as poppadoms and naan, and suggests soups, veg sticks with dips, chicken tikka or grilled fish as healthier starters than spring rolls, samosas and pakoras.
  3. Swap the drink. Choose a sugar-free drink rather than a sugary one. Diabetes UK says plain water is best.
  4. Share rich dishes. Diabetes UK suggests sharing a pizza or a portion of dhal to help manage portion sizes.
  5. Fit in a walk. Diabetes UK suggests a brisk walk before or after the meal to help keep blood sugar levels stable.

If you use insulin

Diabetes UK notes that estimating the carbs in a restaurant or takeaway meal is harder than usual, because you don’t know exactly how the dish was made. It also says dishes such as pizza, curry and fried fish and chips may take hours to affect your blood glucose levels.

If you use insulin or other diabetes medication, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian about how to plan for takeaways and meals out.

Frequently asked questions

Can I still have a takeaway if I have diabetes?

Yes. Diabetes UK says having diabetes shouldn't stop you enjoying eating out, and its tips focus on choosing portion sizes, cooking methods and sides carefully.

What is the healthiest Indian takeaway for diabetes?

Diabetes UK suggests tandoori and tikka dishes because they are baked and lower in fat, with boiled or steamed rice and wholemeal chapatti or roti instead of pilau rice and naan.

Do takeaways have to show calories?

Diabetes UK says restaurants, cafes and takeaways that employ more than 250 people have to display calories on their menus. Smaller local takeaways may not, so ask if you're unsure.
