**Can you enjoy Christmas dinner with diabetes?** Yes. Diabetes UK says Christmas is a time to enjoy yourself, and a short break from your usual routine is normal. A little planning, sensible portions and an eye on the extras, such as sauces, pigs in blankets and drinks, will help you enjoy the day and get back on track afterwards.

## Why does Christmas food need a little thought?

Diabetes UK points out that many traditional Christmas foods tend to be higher in saturated fat, free (added) sugars and salt than our usual diets. That doesn't mean they're off the menu. It just helps to know where the sugar, salt and fat tend to hide.

Diabetes UK also says one or two high readings shouldn't affect your long-term health or diabetes management, but you shouldn't let your blood sugar stay high for long. It suggests testing more often over Christmas if you test, so you can catch changes sooner.

## How do I plan ahead for the holidays?

A few practical steps from Diabetes UK make the festive period easier:

1. **Stock up on medicines.** Make sure you have all the medications you need while your GP surgery is shut, and enough ketone test strips if you test for ketones.
2. **Know where to get help.** If you need urgent medical advice while your GP surgery is closed, contact NHS 111.
3. **Plan around eating out.** Check the menu and nutritional information online before you go. If you're unsure how to fit a big meal around your medication or insulin, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian before the day.
4. **Keep moving.** Diabetes UK says being active can help lower your blood sugar, blood pressure and blood fats over Christmas. A brisk walk, dancing or active party games all count.

## What should go on my Christmas dinner plate?

The good news is that the centrepiece is a sensible choice. The British Heart Foundation (BHF) says turkey is a lean meat, as long as you avoid basting it in too much butter.

| Part of the meal | What the experts suggest |
|---|---|
| Turkey | A lean meat; go easy on the butter when basting (BHF) |
| Vegetables | Fill your plate with them first, and try steaming instead of frying (Diabetes UK) |
| Roast potatoes and parsnips | Go easy, as they add fat and calories (BHF) |
| Pigs in blankets, stuffing and gravy | Usually high in salt (BHF); try to limit processed meat (Diabetes UK) |
| Cranberry and other sauces | Watch for hidden sugars (Diabetes UK) |
| Pudding | Stick to one portion and think about what it's served with (Diabetes UK) |

Diabetes UK suggests serving yourself where you can, so you're in control of how much starchy food, protein and fat you have. It also says to watch out for festive extras like honey on vegetables, because these add free sugars.

## Where are the hidden sugars and salt?

Condiments are easy to forget. Diabetes UK highlights the hidden sugars in cranberry sauce, mint sauce and prawn cocktail sauce, so a spoonful rather than a dollop is worth considering.

Salt adds up too. The BHF says sausages wrapped in bacon, stuffing and gravy are usually high in salt, and too much salt can raise your blood pressure. Diabetes UK suggests using reduced-salt stock cubes for gravy, choosing unsalted nuts and checking the labels on snacks. Our guide to [how much salt a day](/how-much-salt-a-day-high-blood-pressure/) explains the daily limit.

Diabetes UK also suggests trying to limit processed meats such as pigs in blankets, gammon, hams, pâtés and cured meats.

## How can I enjoy Christmas pudding and treats?

You don't have to skip dessert. Diabetes UK suggests sticking to one portion and swapping double cream for natural yoghurt. Its recipe ideas include a Christmas pudding with no free sugars added and "topless" mince pies made with wholemeal pastry flour, which are lower in fat and calories.

The BHF says it's fine to enjoy the odd mince pie or chocolate. It suggests keeping to a regular pattern of meals and snacks so you don't get too hungry, and notes that savoury nibbles like dry-roasted nuts and crisps are high in fat and often high in salt.

For a closer look at sugar limits, see our guide on [how much sugar a day](/how-much-sugar-a-day-uk/).

## What about Christmas drinks?

Diabetes UK says you can drink alcohol if you have diabetes, unless your diabetes team has advised you not to. The UK guideline for men and women is not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread over three days or more.

A few tips from Diabetes UK and the BHF:

1. **Don't drink on an empty stomach.** Diabetes UK says this increases the risk of a hypo if you use insulin or some other diabetes medications.
2. **Check your mixers.** Diabetes UK says a mixer with less than 2.5g of sugar per 100ml is low in sugar, and "light" doesn't always mean low sugar.
3. **Alternate drinks.** The BHF suggests alternating alcoholic drinks with water or sugar-free drinks.
4. **Know the calories.** Diabetes UK says a large glass of wine is about three units and 225 calories.

Diabetes UK warns that the risk of a hypo can last up to 24 hours after you stop drinking, and that a hypo can be mistaken for being drunk. Make sure the people you're with know you have diabetes and how to help.

## When should someone call 999?

The NHS says to call 999 if someone has very low blood sugar and is not responding normally or is unconscious, and a glucagon injection is not available, they haven't recovered within 10 minutes of a glucagon injection, or they've been drinking alcohol. For urgent advice that isn't an emergency, contact NHS 111.

If you get hypos often or have had them at night, the NHS suggests seeing your GP or speaking to your diabetes care team.

## How do I get back on track after Christmas?

Diabetes UK says the festive period shouldn't affect your long-term health or diabetes management as long as you plan for it and get back to your usual habits as soon as possible. A walk on Boxing Day is a good start.

The BHF also suggests eating a bit lighter at other meals, such as soup or a salad, if you've had a big meal earlier in the day.

Buffets and restaurant meals don't come with labels. The Dietary Insight app can estimate the sugar, salt and fat in a plate from a photo and suggest a healthier swap. It's an estimate, so use it alongside your healthcare team's advice.
