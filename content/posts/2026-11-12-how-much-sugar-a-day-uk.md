**How much sugar a day is OK in the UK?** The NHS says adults should have no more than 30g of free sugars a day, roughly 7 sugar cubes. Free sugars should make up no more than 5% of the energy (calories) you get from food and drink, and children's limits are lower.

## What counts as free sugars?

The daily limit applies to free sugars, not to every gram of sugar in your food. The NHS says free sugars include:

- Any sugar added to food or drinks, such as in biscuits, chocolate, flavoured yoghurts, breakfast cereals and fizzy drinks.
- Honey, syrups (such as maple, agave and golden syrup) and nectars.
- Unsweetened fruit juices, vegetable juices and smoothies. The sugars in these occur naturally but still count as free sugars.

Sugar found naturally in milk, whole fruit and vegetables does not count as free sugars. The NHS says the sugar in fruit only becomes free sugar when the fruit is juiced or puréed.

## How much sugar a day for adults and children?

These are the NHS maximum daily amounts of free sugars.

| Age | Maximum free sugars a day | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| Adults and children 11 and over | 30g | 7 sugar cubes |
| 7 to 10 years | 24g | 6 sugar cubes |
| 4 to 6 years | 19g | 5 sugar cubes |
| 2 to 3 years | 14g | 3.5 sugar cubes |
| 1 year | 10g | 2.5 sugar cubes |

There's no guideline limit for babies under 1, but the NHS says sugars should not be added to food or drinks given to babies.

NHS Healthier Families says children are having over 2 times more sugar than is recommended, with half of it coming from snacks and sugary drinks.

## Why does too much sugar matter?

The NHS says eating too much sugar can make you gain weight and can also cause tooth decay. Sugar adds calories, and too many calories can lead to weight gain.

Being overweight raises your risk of health problems such as heart disease, some cancers and type 2 diabetes, according to the NHS. Sugar is also one of the main causes of tooth decay, which is why the NHS suggests keeping sugary foods to mealtimes.

## How quickly does sugar add up?

Free sugars hide in everyday foods and drinks. Here are some examples from the NHS:

| Food or drink | Sugar |
|---|---|
| Can of cola | Up to 9 cubes, more than the adult daily limit |
| Chocolate bar | Up to 24g (6 cubes) |
| Sugary yoghurt pot | Up to 24g (6 cubes) |
| Cereal bar | 12g (3 cubes) |
| 150ml glass of fruit juice | Around 15g |

Even unsweetened fruit juice and smoothies are sugary. The NHS says to keep them to a combined 150ml a day, drunk with a meal. They only count as one of your 5 A Day, however much you have. ## How do you spot sugar on food labels?

The "of which sugars" figure on a label shows total sugars: free sugars plus the sugars from milk, fruit and vegetables. The NHS gives these guides per 100g:

- **High:** more than 22.5g of total sugars per 100g.
- **Low:** 5g of total sugars or less per 100g.
- **Medium:** anything in between.

On front-of-pack labels, red means high, amber medium and green low. NHS Healthier Families suggests picking items with more greens and ambers and cutting down on ones with reds.

Because the label shows total sugars, some foods look higher than they are. The NHS says plain yoghurt may contain as much as 8g of sugar per serving, but none of it is free sugar.

The ingredients list helps too. The NHS says that if sugar is near the top of the list, the food is likely to be high in free sugars. The NHS says added sugar can appear under many names, including:

1. Sucrose, glucose, fructose, dextrose and maltose.
2. Cane sugar, brown sugar and crystalline sucrose.
3. Corn syrup and high-fructose corn syrup.
4. Honey, molasses and treacle.
5. Maple syrup, agave syrup and nectars.
6. Fruit juice concentrates and purées.

## What are easy ways to cut down on sugar?

The NHS suggests small swaps that add up:

- Drink water, lower-fat milk, or sugar-free, diet or no-added-sugar drinks instead of sugary fizzy drinks or squash.
- If you take sugar in hot drinks or add it to breakfast cereal, gradually reduce the amount.
- Try sliced banana on toast in place of high-sugar spreads.
- Choose unsweetened wholegrain breakfast cereals that are not frosted.
- Buy tinned fruit in juice rather than syrup.
- Try reducing the sugar in your recipes. The NHS says it works for most things except jam, meringues and ice cream.
- Keep sweets and treats to mealtimes to help protect teeth.

If you have diabetes and want help with how sugar and carbohydrates fit into your meals, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian.
