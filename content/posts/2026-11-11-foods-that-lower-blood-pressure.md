**Foods that can help lower blood pressure** include fruit and vegetables, wholegrains such as oats, lean protein and low-fat dairy, according to the British Heart Foundation (BHF). What you cut back on matters just as much: less salt, less alcohol and fewer sugary, fatty foods. No single food does the job, but the overall pattern of your plate can make a real difference.

## Why does food make a difference to blood pressure?

The NHS lists an unhealthy diet, especially one that's high in salt, as one of the things that raises your risk of high blood pressure. It also says healthy lifestyle changes can help lower your blood pressure, and that lowering it even a small amount can help reduce your risk of serious health problems.

The BHF describes diet as one of the tools you can use to help keep blood pressure under control. It sits alongside being active, not smoking, keeping to a healthy weight and taking any medicines you've been prescribed.

## Which foods can help lower blood pressure?

Here's a quick summary of the BHF's advice on what to include more often.

| Food group | Examples | Why it's recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit and vegetables | Bananas, spinach, kale, celery, strawberries, beetroot | Supply potassium, which can help lower blood pressure |
| Wholegrains | Oats, brown rice, wholemeal bread | Fibre; oat beta-glucans may help |
| Lean protein | Chicken, turkey, fish, eggs, beans | A swap for red and processed meats |
| Low-fat dairy | Semi-skimmed milk, low-fat natural yogurt | Calcium is linked to lower blood pressure |

### Fruit and vegetables

The BHF says all fruit and vegetables supply potassium, which can help lower blood pressure, and suggests eating at least five portions every day. Blood Pressure UK says one portion is 80g.

Some vegetables also contain nitrates. The BHF says research has suggested beetroot juice might help lower blood pressure, and that spinach, celery, kale, bananas and strawberries are other sources of nitrates.

### Wholegrains and fibre

Swap white versions for wholegrains such as brown rice, wholemeal bread and oats. The BHF says the soluble fibre in oats, called beta-glucans, may help in lowering blood pressure. Beans, lentils, nuts and seeds are good sources of fibre too.

### Lean protein and low-fat dairy

The BHF suggests choosing chicken, turkey, fish, eggs and beans instead of red and processed meats. It also says milk and dairy could help with lowering blood pressure, as calcium is linked to blood pressure reduction. Semi-skimmed milk and low-fat natural yogurt are easy choices.

## What counts as a portion of fruit and veg?

Blood Pressure UK gives these examples of one 80g portion:

1. A small bowl of salad.
2. Three heaped tablespoons of vegetables, or of beans and pulses.
3. One medium fruit, or two smaller fruits.
4. Two to three tablespoons of berries or grapes.
5. One tablespoon of dried fruit, best eaten at mealtimes.
6. A 150ml glass of fruit juice, which only counts once a day however much you drink.

Fresh, frozen, tinned and dried all count. Choose tinned fruit in juice or water, with no added sugar or salt. Potatoes, yams, cassava and plantain don't count, but sweet potatoes, parsnips, swedes and turnips do.

## Which foods are rich in potassium?

Blood Pressure UK lists these potassium-rich foods:

- **Fruit:** bananas, apricots, currants, orange juice, tomato juice and tomato purée.
- **Vegetables:** potatoes, sweet potatoes, asparagus, spinach, cabbage and sprouts.
- **Other foods:** salmon, tuna tinned in spring water, yogurt, fat-free milk, eggs, almonds, mushrooms and wholemeal pasta.

Get your potassium from food, not tablets. The BHF says too much potassium can affect the rhythm of the heart, and that this is especially dangerous for people with kidney problems or taking certain water tablets. Only take potassium supplements if your doctor has prescribed them.

Blood Pressure UK adds that if you have kidney disease or take certain blood pressure medicines, you should check with your doctor before greatly increasing the potassium in your diet. It also says to avoid potassium-based salt substitutes in that case. If you have diabetes or kidney problems, Blood Pressure UK says to check with your doctor or nurse before using a low-sodium salt substitute.

## Which foods should you limit?

### Salt

The BHF says we should have no more than 6g of salt a day, which is about a teaspoon. Much of it is hidden in everyday foods such as bread, breakfast cereals, sauces, crisps, olives, pickles and processed meats. Our guide to [how much salt a day](/how-much-salt-a-day-high-blood-pressure/) shows how to spot it on labels.

### Alcohol

Too much alcohol can raise blood pressure. The BHF suggests staying well within the guideline of no more than 14 units a week, with some alcohol-free days.

### Sugary and fatty foods

The BHF says sugar and fat may not directly affect blood pressure, but the weight gain they can cause is linked to raised blood pressure. For snacks, it suggests fruit, plain yogurt, nuts and dried fruit.

### Caffeine

The BHF says a moderate intake, 4 to 5 cups a day, should not be a problem for most people. Coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate all contain caffeine.

## Can food replace blood pressure medicine?

Don't stop or change any medicine because you've changed what you eat. The BHF says it's important to take any medicines you've been prescribed, and healthy eating works alongside them. If you have questions about your blood pressure or your treatment, talk to your GP, practice nurse or a dietitian.

## When should you get help?

The NHS says to get help from NHS 111 if you often have headaches or blurred vision, have chest pain that comes and goes, or have other symptoms you're worried could be caused by high blood pressure.

**Call 999** if you get sudden chest pain or discomfort that does not go away, pain that spreads to your arms, neck, jaw, stomach or back, or chest pain with feeling sweaty, sick, light-headed or short of breath. The NHS says you could be having a heart attack. Do not drive yourself to A&E.
