**Is there hidden salt in bread and cereal?** Yes. Diabetes UK lists bread and breakfast cereals among the main sources of salt in our diets, and the British Heart Foundation (BHF) says one slice of thick, wholemeal bread can contain as much as 2g of salt, a third of an adult's daily limit. Checking labels and choosing lower-salt options can make a real difference.

## Why does hidden salt matter?

The recommended maximum for adults is 6g of salt a day, which is about one teaspoon. The BHF says too much salt can raise your blood pressure, which puts extra strain on your heart over time.

Diabetes UK says we're still eating more than that 6g, and that the recommended amount is even less for children. Its list of the main sources of salt includes bread and breakfast cereals, which most people don't think of as salty.

## Why does salt matter if you have diabetes?

Diabetes UK says salt does not affect blood glucose levels. But it's still important to limit it as part of managing diabetes, because too much salt can raise your blood pressure.

Diabetes UK also says people with diabetes are more likely to be affected by high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease, stroke and kidney disease. That's why salt is worth watching alongside sugar and carbohydrate.

## How much salt is in bread?

More than most people expect. The BHF gives the example of one slice of thick, wholemeal bread containing as much as 2g of salt, which is one third of an adult's daily limit.

This isn't a reason to give up bread. It's a reason to compare labels before you buy. The BHF suggests comparing labels and looking for "low sodium" or "reduced salt" on the packaging.

## Which breakfast cereals are high in salt?

The BHF says some cereals, even those making health claims, can contain a surprising amount of salt, which is often added to boost flavour and lengthen shelf life. Here's the salt per serving from the BHF's breakfast cereal ranking:

| Cereal (serving) | Salt per serving |
|---|---|
| Porridge oats, 40g (without milk) | 0.0g |
| Muesli with no added sugar or salt, 45g | 0.01g |
| Whole wheat cereals and biscuits, 45g | 0.02g |
| Muesli with added sugar, 45g | 0.13g |
| Bran flakes, 30g | 0.2g |
| Sugar-frosted cornflakes, 30g | 0.25g |
| Cornflakes, 30g | 0.34g |

The BHF says salt levels vary between brands, so check the nutrition label before you buy. Diabetes UK's own cereal review found that Nestlé Shredded Wheat and Weetabix were green for salt, sugar, fat and saturated fat.

## How do I spot salt on a food label?

The BHF uses these per 100g guides for salt:

| Salt per 100g | What it means |
|---|---|
| More than 1.5g | High |
| 0.3g or less | Low |

Many packs also use traffic light colours: green for low, amber for medium and red for high. Diabetes UK suggests choosing foods where the salt is green or amber, rather than red.

A few tips that help when you're in the supermarket:

1. **Compare like with like.** Diabetes UK says the salt content of foods such as breakfast cereals and sandwiches can vary, so shop around.
2. **Look for reduced-salt versions.** Diabetes UK suggests looking out for reduced-salt options of your favourite foods.
3. **Check the serving size.** Per-serving figures assume the stated portion, so pouring a bigger bowl means more salt.

## What are good lower-salt swaps?

Small changes at breakfast and lunch add up over a week. Ideas based on the BHF's advice:

1. **Start with a salt-free wholegrain cereal.** The BHF suggests wheat biscuits, some types of muesli or porridge oats.
2. **Choose porridge.** The BHF says plain oats have no added sugar or salt.
3. **Swap your usual loaf.** Compare a few brands and pick the one with the lowest salt per 100g, or one labelled "reduced salt".
4. **Go easy on salty toppings.** The BHF lists smoked fish and some cheeses among foods that can be surprisingly high in salt. It also notes low-fat cheese may be saltier than standard cheddar.
5. **Skip salt at the table.** The BHF includes this among its tips for cutting down.

If you have high blood pressure, diabetes or kidney problems and want personalised advice on salt, talk to your GP, practice nurse, diabetes nurse or a dietitian.

## What about foods without a label?

Bread from a bakery counter or a café breakfast doesn't always come with nutrition information. The Dietary Insight app can estimate the sugar, salt and fat in a meal from a photo and suggest a lower-salt swap. It's an estimate, so use it alongside food labels and your healthcare team's advice.
