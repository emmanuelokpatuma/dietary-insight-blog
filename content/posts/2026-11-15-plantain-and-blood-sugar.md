**How does plantain affect blood sugar?** Plantain is a starchy food, so it raises blood sugar like other carbohydrates, but Diabetes UK lists it as an example of a low GI food that raises blood sugar slowly. Your portion size and how you cook it matter more than the GI on its own, so plantain can still be part of a healthy diet when you have diabetes.

## Is plantain a starchy food?

Yes. Diabetes UK says starchy foods are things like potatoes, rice, pasta, bread, chapattis, naan and plantain. Starchy foods contain carbohydrate, and the GI ranks carbohydrate foods by how quickly they raise your blood sugar.

That doesn't mean you need to avoid plantain. Diabetes UK says to try to have some starchy foods every day, and notes that some starchy foods can raise blood glucose levels quickly, so it's worth choosing lower-GI options where you can.

## What is the GI of plantain?

The glycaemic index (GI) ranks carbohydrate foods from 0 to 100 by how quickly they raise blood sugar. Diabetes UK groups foods like this:

| GI score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 55 or below | Low: raises blood sugar slowly |
| 56 to 69 | Medium: raises blood sugar at a medium rate |
| 70 or more | High: raises blood sugar quickly |

Diabetes UK includes plantain and yam in its list of example low GI foods. By comparison, it lists regular potatoes, whether baked, boiled, fried or mashed, as high GI. For more examples, see our [low GI foods list](/low-gi-foods-list-uk/).

Diabetes UK also lists plantain as a lower-GI swap. It suggests choosing brown or basmati rice instead of white rice, or trying plantain, quinoa, bulgur wheat or barley for a change.

## Does ripeness or cooking change how plantain affects blood sugar?

The GI of a food isn't fixed. Diabetes UK says several things can change it, including:

1. The ripeness or variety of fruit and vegetables.
2. Cooking methods, such as frying, boiling and baking.
3. Processing, such as milling or pureeing.
4. Fat and protein, which both lower the GI of a food.

So green plantain and very ripe plantain may not behave in exactly the same way, and boiled, steamed, baked or fried plantain may differ too. Diabetes UK doesn't give separate GI figures for each one, so it's best not to rely on a single number.

A lower GI isn't the whole story. Diabetes UK points out that crisps have a lower GI than potatoes cooked without fat, because fat lowers the GI. That's why the oil you use still matters.

## Is the amount of plantain more important than the GI?

Yes. Diabetes UK says the amount of carbs you eat has a bigger effect on blood sugar levels than GI alone. GI also doesn't take your portion size into account.

Diabetes UK's advice is that the most important thing is to watch your carbohydrate portion sizes. It also notes that larger portions can make it more difficult to manage your weight.

A few practical ways to do this with plantain:

1. Serve it as one part of the plate, not the whole meal.
2. Pair it with beans, fish, chicken or eggs. Diabetes UK says protein, fat and fibre slow down the digestion and absorption of carbohydrates.
3. Add vegetables or a salsa on the side for extra fibre.
4. If you're having plantain with rice, think about the total amount of starchy food on the plate.

If you'd like help working out the right amount of carbohydrate for you, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian.

## What are healthier ways to cook plantain?

Fried plantain, often called dodo, is a favourite in many homes. The UK recipes below show other ways to enjoy it using little or no oil.

| Recipe | How it's cooked | Oil used |
|---|---|---|
| Diabetes UK plantain chips | Thinly sliced green plantain, baked | 2 tsp sunflower oil for 4 servings |
| NHS grilled plantain with fresh salsa | Ripe plantain, grilled | None listed |
| NHS ewa oloyin with plantain | Ripe plantain, steamed | 2 tsp vegetable oil for the whole dish |

When you do use oil, Diabetes UK lists olive oil, vegetable oil and rapeseed oil as healthier fats. It describes butter, palm nut oil and coconut oil as less healthy options.

## What's in a serving of these plantain recipes?

Here are the per-serving figures from each recipe page. Each recipe serves 4.

| Per serving | Diabetes UK plantain chips (162g, includes serving suggestion) | NHS grilled plantain with salsa | NHS ewa oloyin with plantain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy | 200kcal | 94kcal | 265kcal |
| Carbohydrate | 26.5g | 23g | 41g |
| of which sugars | 8.0g | 6g | 9.1g |
| Fibre | 7.7g | 1.5g | 5.4g |
| Protein | 7.0g | 1g | 11g |
| Fat | 5.6g | 0.3g | 4.9g |
| Salt | 0.5g | Trace | 0.7g |

The ewa oloyin includes beans, which add protein and fibre. The NHS recipe also says you could swap the plantain for boiled yam or sweet potato. Its salt is listed as optional, so you can leave it out.

The Diabetes UK plantain chips are pitched as a healthier swap for a packet of crisps. They're baked at 190°C (170°C fan, gas 5) for 15 to 18 minutes and served with a tomato, pineapple and chilli salsa.

## Can plantain fit into a diabetes-friendly African or Caribbean meal?

Yes. Diabetes UK names plantain among everyday starchy foods and suggests it as a change from white rice, so it doesn't need to come off the menu. NHS Healthier Families describes grilled ripe plantain as a common snack in many West African countries.

## Checking your plate when there's no label

Home-cooked plantain and party food don't come with a nutrition label. The Dietary Insight app can estimate the sugar, salt and fat in a meal from a photo and suggest a healthier swap. It gives an estimate, so use it alongside food labels and your healthcare team's advice.
