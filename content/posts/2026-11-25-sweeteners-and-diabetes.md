**Can you use sweeteners if you have diabetes?** Yes. Diabetes UK says low-calorie sweeteners give you sweetness while reducing your sugar and calorie intake, because they contain little or no calories or carbohydrate and don't affect blood glucose levels. Using them is a personal choice, and they work best as one part of a balanced diet rather than a fix on their own.

## What are sweeteners?

The NHS describes lower or no calorie sweeteners as substances used instead of sugar to sweeten foods and drinks. You'll find them in drinks, desserts, ready meals, cakes, chewing gum and even toothpaste.

Diabetes UK says it is more useful to split sweeteners into two groups than to worry about "natural" versus "artificial":

| Type | Examples | Carbs and calories? |
|---|---|---|
| Nutritive sweeteners | Sugar (sucrose), glucose, fructose, honey, syrup | Yes, they contain carbohydrate and calories |
| Low-calorie (non-nutritive) sweeteners | Aspartame, sucralose, saccharin, acesulfame K, stevia-based sweeteners | Little or none |
| Sugar alcohols (polyols) | Sorbitol, xylitol, erythritol, maltitol, isomalt | Some, but fewer calories and less effect on blood glucose than sugar |

Diabetes UK points out that labels such as "natural" can be confusing. Stevia products sold as natural are still processed, and sucralose is made from sugar.

## Do sweeteners affect blood sugar?

Diabetes UK says low-calorie sweeteners don't affect blood glucose levels. A joint position statement from the British Dietetic Association (BDA), the British Nutrition Foundation and Diabetes UK says replacing free sugars, including those in sugary drinks, with low or no calorie sweeteners can be an effective way to reduce carbohydrate intake and manage blood glucose after meals.

The same statement adds that sweeteners are not a stand-alone solution. A balanced diet lower in sugars, saturated fat and salt, and high in fibre, is still what matters most. It also says water is the ideal drink.

Sugar alcohols are a little different. Diabetes UK says they contain carbohydrate and calories, but have less effect on blood glucose than sugar. Because not all of that carbohydrate is absorbed, it's unclear how they should be counted if you adjust insulin to the carbs you eat, so speak to your healthcare team for individual advice.

## Are sweeteners safe?

The NHS says all sweeteners in Great Britain go through a rigorous safety assessment before they can be used in food and drink, and all approved sweeteners are considered a safe and acceptable alternative to sugar. The law also controls how much sweetener can go into which products.

Each sweetener has an acceptable daily intake (ADI). Diabetes UK explains that ADIs are set 100 times below the smallest amount that may cause health concerns, and that it's extremely difficult for most people to reach them. The NHS says you do not need to keep track of how much sweetener you have each day.

To put that in context, the joint position statement says an adult with no other source of aspartame could, in theory, drink about 12 cans a day of a soft drink sweetened at the maximum permitted level. It stresses this is a theoretical upper limit, not a recommended amount.

The NHS adds that Cancer Research UK has said sweeteners do not cause cancer, and that there is no evidence sweeteners stimulate appetite in humans. It describes the wider evidence on sweeteners and health as mixed and limited.

### Who should avoid aspartame?

People with phenylketonuria (PKU), a rare inherited condition, should avoid aspartame. The NHS says products containing it must list aspartame or E951 in the ingredients and carry a warning that they contain a source of phenylalanine.

## What about "diabetic" foods and sugar alcohols?

Diabetes UK says sugar alcohols are usually used in products marketed as "diabetic" or "suitable for diabetics". It does not recommend these products, because they can be as high in fat and calories as standard versions.

Large amounts of sugar alcohols can also cause bloating, wind and diarrhoea. The NHS says that if added polyols make up more than 10% of a product, the label must warn that excessive consumption may produce laxative effects.

## Are sweeteners suitable for children?

The joint position statement says the use of sweeteners is not allowed in any foods made for children under 36 months, and that young children should not be encouraged to have products with sweeteners. For children aged 5 and over, it says sweeteners may be an option to help cut excess sugar, depending on their needs. Water and milk are the recommended drinks for children.

## How to use sweeteners sensibly

Based on the UK guidance, a few practical tips:

1. **Swap sugary drinks first.** The joint statement says sugar-sweetened drinks are a major source of free sugars, and diet versions may be a useful alternative for regular drinkers. Water is still the best choice.
2. **Check the whole label.** Diabetes UK says "sugar-free" products aren't necessarily low in calories, so look at fat and calories too. Our guide to [reading food labels with diabetes](/how-to-read-food-labels-diabetes/) explains what to look for.
3. **Choose the right one for cooking.** Diabetes UK says aspartame loses some sweetness at high temperatures, while sucralose and acesulfame K can be used in cooking and baking.
4. **Don't "save" calories elsewhere.** The joint statement warns against eating larger portions or less healthy foods on the assumption that sweeteners have saved calories.
5. **Reduce sweetness overall.** Diabetes UK says everyone should be eating less sugar, but people with diabetes don't need a completely sugar-free diet. See [how much sugar a day](/how-much-sugar-a-day-uk/) is recommended in the UK.

If you're not sure whether sweeteners suit you, or how they fit with your medication, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian.
