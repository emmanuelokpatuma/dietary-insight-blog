**Natasha's Law is the UK allergen labelling rule for food that is made and packed on the same premises where it's sold.** Since 1 October 2021, this "prepacked for direct sale" (PPDS) food must carry a label with the name of the food and a full ingredients list, with the 14 major allergens emphasised. It covers things like sandwiches, salad boxes and wrapped deli cheese that are packed before you order.

## What is Natasha's Law?

Natasha's Law is the common name for a change to UK food labelling law that came into force on 1 October 2021. The Food Standards Agency (FSA) says the labelling requirements for PPDS food changed across the UK from that date.

Allergy UK explains that the law followed the death of teenager Natasha Ednan-Laperouse in 2016. She died from an allergic reaction caused by an ingredient in a baguette bought at a food outlet at Heathrow Airport. At the inquest, the coroner said the legislation at the time was not adequate and asked for a review.

## What does "prepacked for direct sale" mean?

The FSA describes PPDS food as food that is packaged at the same place it is offered or sold to consumers, and is ready to hand over before it is ordered or selected.

Food usually counts as PPDS when it is fully or partly enclosed by packaging, can't be changed without opening the packaging, and is ready for sale. The FSA gives examples such as a cake in cling film, sandwiches in a folded paper bag and rolls in a knotted or sealed plastic bag.

### Examples of PPDS food

- Prepacked sandwiches, wraps and salad boxes
- Pies in packaging and soup already in pots
- Prepacked paninis or boxes of pizza that can be reheated when you ask
- Cheeses and meats from a supermarket deli counter that have already been wrapped (Allergy UK)

## What must a PPDS label show?

The FSA says labels on PPDS food must show the name of the food and the ingredients list. Any of the 14 allergens in the ingredients must be emphasised, for example in bold, capital letters, a contrasting colour or underlined text.

Allergy UK adds that an allergen must be emphasised even if the ingredient comes under a different name. Its own example shows whey powder listed as "whey powder (from milk)" in bold.

The 14 allergens that must be emphasised are:

| The 14 allergens | |
|---|---|
| Celery | Milk |
| Cereals containing gluten | Molluscs |
| Crustaceans | Mustard |
| Eggs | Nuts |
| Fish | Peanuts |
| Lupin | Sesame seeds |
| Soya | Sulphur dioxide and sulphites |

## What food isn't covered by Natasha's Law?

Not every food you buy in a café or shop is PPDS. The FSA explains how the main types differ:

| Type of food | Example | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| PPDS | A sandwich made and packed on site before you order | Full label with name, ingredients and allergens emphasised |
| Prepacked elsewhere | A sandwich made in a factory and delivered to the shop | Full label with name, ingredients, allergens and other details |
| Non-prepacked or made to order | Unpackaged cakes on display, an open salad bar, food made to order or packed when you ask | No label needed, but allergen information must still be available |
| Distance selling | Food ordered online or by phone | PPDS label rules don't apply, but allergen information must be given before you order and on delivery |

The FSA also says hot drinks made to order are not PPDS, while pre-poured drinks would be PPDS and need a label. For loose food, such as a buffet, a business can list allergens on menus or display a sign telling you to ask staff.

## What about "may contain" labels?

The FSA describes a precautionary allergen label as a statement a business can choose to add when there is an unavoidable risk of allergen cross-contamination. It is often written as "may contain" an allergen or "not suitable for someone with" an allergy.

Allergy UK says there is currently no law on when these statements should be used. It advises that guidance on whether you need to avoid foods with these statements should come from a healthcare professional.

## How can you use Natasha's Law when shopping?

A full ingredients list on a PPDS label gives you more to go on, but it still works best alongside a few habits:

1. **Read the label every time.** Allergy UK says food labels need to be checked every time a product is bought.
2. **Check new products carefully.** Always check the label on anything you haven't bought before.
3. **Ask about loose food.** For unpackaged food, ask staff about ingredients and what precautions are taken against cross-contamination.
4. **If in doubt, leave it.** Allergy UK says if the label isn't clear, or you still have doubts after speaking to staff, don't take the risk.

For home-cooked meals and food with no label, the Dietary Insight app can flag ingredients in a photo of a dish that may contain allergens on your profile, as a starting point for your questions. It can't see hidden ingredients or cross-contamination, so always check the full label and ask the person who made the food.

## When should you call 999?

The NHS says to call 999 if you think you or someone else is having an anaphylactic reaction. Signs include lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swelling, breathing very fast or struggling to breathe, a tight throat or trouble swallowing, skin, tongue or lips turning blue, grey or pale, suddenly feeling very confused, drowsy or dizzy, or someone fainting and not waking up. A child may be limp, floppy or not responding as usual.

If you have an adrenaline auto-injector, the NHS says to use it, call 999 and say you think it's anaphylaxis. Use a second auto-injector if symptoms haven't improved after 5 minutes. Lie down and raise your legs, or sit up slowly if you're struggling to breathe, and don't stand or walk, even if you feel better.

If you're unsure whether you have a food allergy, or what you should avoid, talk to your GP.
