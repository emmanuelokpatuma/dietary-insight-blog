**Can you eat jollof rice with diabetes?** Yes. Jollof rice can still be part of a healthy diet when you have diabetes, and a few swaps make a real difference: brown basmati instead of white rice, a reduced-salt stock cube, a measured spoon of oil, plenty of vegetables and a sensible portion. Diabetes UK even has its own jollof rice and chicken recipe built around these ideas.

## Why does jollof rice affect blood sugar?

Rice is a starchy food, and starchy foods are mostly carbohydrate. Diabetes UK lists rice alongside potatoes, pasta, bread, chapattis and plantain as starchy foods.

Diabetes UK says the amount of carbs you eat has a bigger effect on blood sugar levels than the glycaemic index (GI) alone. So the size of your portion matters most, followed by the type of rice you choose.

That doesn't mean jollof is off the menu. The NHS calls rice and grains an excellent choice of starchy food, and suggests choosing wholegrain varieties where you can. For more on rice in general, see our guide [is rice bad for diabetes?](/is-rice-bad-for-diabetes/)

## Which rice is best for jollof if you have diabetes?

Diabetes UK suggests choosing brown rice or basmati rice instead of white rice. Brown basmati is a wholegrain, and wholegrains add fibre, which Diabetes UK says acts as a physical barrier that slows down the absorption of carbohydrate.

Diabetes UK groups foods by how quickly they raise blood sugar:

| GI score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 55 or below | Raises blood sugar slowly |
| 56 to 69 | Raises blood sugar at a medium rate |
| 70 or more | Raises blood sugar quickly |

Diabetes UK also notes that protein and fat lower the GI of a food. That's one reason a jollof cooked with chicken, fish or beans and plenty of vegetables is a better choice than a large plate of rice on its own.

If you usually cook with easy-cook white rice, NHS Healthier Families says you could use easy-cook brown rice instead, but it will take an extra 10 to 15 minutes to cook. Keep an eye on the liquid as it simmers.

## What are the best healthier jollof swaps?

These swaps come from the Diabetes UK and NHS jollof recipes and their wider healthy eating advice.

| Instead of | Try | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Long grain white rice | Brown basmati rice | Diabetes UK suggests brown or basmati rice instead of white |
| A regular stock cube | A reduced-salt stock cube | The NHS suggests low or reduced-salt stock cubes to cut salt |
| Free-pouring oil | 2 tsp rapeseed oil for 4 servings | Diabetes UK lists rapeseed oil as a healthier fat; its recipe uses 2 tsp |
| Extra salt | Garlic, ginger, Scotch bonnet and herbs | The NHS suggests herbs, spices, black pepper, garlic, chilli or lemon juice instead of salt |
| Rice on its own | Peppers, okra and tomatoes cooked in | More vegetables towards your 5 A Day |
| Fatty cuts of meat | Chicken breast, fish, prawns or tofu | Diabetes UK's recipe uses chicken breast and suggests other options |

Salt is worth watching because stock cubes and seasoning add up fast. The NHS says adults should have no more than 6g of salt a day, around 1 level teaspoon. Our guide to [how much salt a day](/how-much-salt-a-day-high-blood-pressure/) explains where hidden salt comes from.

## What does a healthier serving of jollof look like?

Diabetes UK's jollof rice and chicken recipe serves 4. It uses brown basmati rice, rapeseed oil, a reduced-salt chicken stock cube, chicken breast, peppers, tomatoes and okra. Here is the nutrition for each 393g serving, as given by Diabetes UK:

| Per serving (393g) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Energy | 377kcal |
| Carbohydrate | 50.3g |
| Fibre | 7.1g |
| Protein | 29.7g |
| Fat | 5.1g |
| Saturates | 0.8g (low) |
| Sugars | 11.9g (low) |
| Salt | 0.28g (low) |
| Fruit and veg portions | 3 |

Diabetes UK suggests you could make it with lamb, beef, pork or turkey instead of chicken. For a vegetarian or vegan version, it suggests courgette, aubergine and marinated tofu. For a seafood version, it suggests a fish stock cube with salmon or prawns.

## How much jollof rice should you eat?

There's no single right portion for everyone. Diabetes UK points out that portion sizes have grown as the plates and bowls we use have got bigger, and larger portions can make it more difficult to manage your weight.

A few practical ways to keep portions in check:

1. Remember the NHS guide that starchy foods should make up just over a third of the food you eat, so rice doesn't need to fill the whole plate.
2. Serve jollof with grilled chicken, fish or meat and some brightly coloured steamed vegetables or a side salad, as NHS Healthier Families suggests.
3. Add frozen vegetables to the pan towards the end of cooking, another NHS Healthier Families tip, to bulk out each portion.
4. Use a smaller plate or bowl, since bigger plates have helped portions grow.

If you'd like help working out the right amount of rice or carbohydrate for you, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian.

## Why does this matter for African and Caribbean communities?

Diabetes UK describes jollof as a popular West African dish, and a healthier version is easy to share with the whole household. Diabetes UK says people from Black African, African Caribbean and South Asian backgrounds are at risk of developing type 2 diabetes from the age of 25, much younger than the white population, whose risk increases from 40.

Diabetes UK also says carrying extra weight is the biggest risk factor for type 2 diabetes. It suggests making healthier food choices, such as eating more fruit and vegetables and choosing wholegrains.

## How do you store and reheat leftover jollof safely?

Jollof often feeds a crowd, so leftovers are common. The NHS warns that if cooked rice or grains are left standing at room temperature, spores can germinate. The NHS also says rice should not be reheated more than once. After that, it should be thrown away.
