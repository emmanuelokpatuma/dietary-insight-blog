**Type 2 diabetes symptoms** most often include feeling very tired, peeing more than usual, feeling thirsty all the time and losing weight without trying. They may come on gradually, and many people have no symptoms at all, so the NHS says it's important to get checked if you think you're at risk.

## What are the most common symptoms of type 2 diabetes?

The NHS lists four main symptoms:

1. Feeling very tired
2. Peeing more than usual
3. Feeling thirsty all the time
4. Losing weight without trying to

Other symptoms the NHS mentions are blurred vision, cuts or wounds taking longer to heal, and itching around the penis or vagina, or getting thrush again and again. The NHS says these symptoms are the same for adults and children.

## What are the 4 Ts of diabetes?

Diabetes UK uses the 4 Ts as an easy way to remember the main signs of diabetes:

| The T | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Toilet | Going for a wee a lot, especially at night |
| Thirsty | Being really thirsty |
| Tired | Feeling more tired than usual |
| Thinner | Losing weight without trying to |

Diabetes UK also lists increased hunger, behaviour changes such as feeling more irritable, genital itching or thrush, cuts and wounds taking longer to heal, and blurred eyesight. It explains that these symptoms happen when there isn't enough insulin to move glucose (sugar) from the blood into the cells.

Having some of these symptoms doesn't mean you definitely have diabetes, Diabetes UK says. But it is a good reason to speak to your GP.

## Can you have type 2 diabetes without knowing?

Yes, and this is one of the most important things to know. The NHS says not everyone with type 2 diabetes has symptoms, and when symptoms do appear they may develop gradually.

Diabetes UK says type 2 diabetes can be easier to miss because it develops more slowly, especially in the early stages. It can go undiagnosed for years if you don't have symptoms or your symptoms are missed.

That's why knowing your risk matters as much as knowing the symptoms. If you've been told you have raised blood sugar, our guide to [what prediabetes is](/what-is-prediabetes-can-it-be-reversed/) explains what that means.

## Who is more likely to get type 2 diabetes?

The NHS says you're more likely to get type 2 diabetes if:

- you're white and over 40, or over 25 and from a South Asian, Chinese, Black African or Black Caribbean background
- a parent, brother or sister has it
- you're overweight and not very physically active
- you have a condition that increases your risk, such as high blood pressure

Diabetes UK adds that people from African Caribbean, Black African or South Asian backgrounds are at higher risk, often from a younger age. It also lists your waist measurement and genetics among the risk factors, and says risk increases as you get older.

Type 2 diabetes doesn't only affect people living with overweight or obesity, Diabetes UK says, although that is one of the risk factors.

## How do I check if I have type 2 diabetes?

The NHS suggests using Diabetes UK's free Know Your Risk tool, which Diabetes UK says takes about two minutes. If your score is high or very high, Diabetes UK says you'll need to ask your GP for a blood test.

If you're between 40 and 74 and don't have an existing health condition, the NHS says you may also be able to get a free NHS Health Check at your GP surgery or a local pharmacy.

Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed with a blood test that checks how high your blood glucose levels are. The NHS says results usually take a few days.

## When should you see a GP?

The NHS says to see a GP if you think you or your child may have type 2 diabetes, or if you or your child have a higher risk of getting it.

Diabetes UK says that if you feel very unwell, or your symptoms of diabetes have come on quickly, it's essential to make an urgent appointment with your GP or call NHS 111.

### When to call 999

The NHS high blood sugar page says to call 999 or go to A&E if you think you have high blood sugar and:

- you're feeling sick, being sick or have stomach pain
- you're breathing more quickly than usual or your heart is beating faster than usual
- you feel drowsy or are struggling to stay awake
- your breath has a fruity smell (like pear drop sweets)
- you feel confused or have difficulty concentrating
- you have a high level of ketones in your blood or pee

The NHS says these could be signs you're becoming seriously unwell. Don't drive yourself to A&E: ask someone to drive you, or call 999 for an ambulance.

## Why getting diagnosed early matters

Diabetes UK says untreated diabetes can harm many major organs, including the heart, blood vessels, nerves, eyes and kidneys. It adds that being diagnosed early and managing your blood sugar levels can help prevent these complications.

If you've already been diagnosed and have questions about your treatment, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian.
