**What fruit can diabetics eat?** Any fruit you like. Diabetes UK says there's no reason people with diabetes can't eat any fruit, as long as it's whole fruit spread through the day. The things that matter most are portion size, keeping dried fruit small and limiting fruit juice and smoothies to 150ml a day.

## Can people with diabetes eat fruit?

Yes. Diabetes UK puts it plainly: having diabetes doesn't mean you can't have fruit. Fresh, frozen, dried and tinned fruit all count towards your 5 A Day.

Fruit gives you fibre, which Diabetes UK says is good for your overall health. It does also contain carbohydrate in the form of sugar, and that affects your blood glucose levels. That's why how much you eat in one go matters more than which fruit you choose.

## Is the sugar in fruit bad for diabetes?

The sugar in whole fruit is treated differently from the sugar in biscuits or fizzy drinks. Diabetes UK says the sugar naturally found in whole fruit does not count towards free sugar, because your body uses it differently.

Diabetes UK also notes that most fruits have a low to medium glycaemic index (GI). That means they don't have the same immediate effect on blood sugar as refined carbs like white bread. If you want to know more about GI, our [low GI foods list](/low-gi-foods-list-uk/) explains how it works.

Juice is a different story, and we cover it below.

## Which fruits are best for diabetes?

There's no list of banned fruits. Diabetes UK's advice covers any fruit you like, including bananas, grapes, mango and pineapple. Instead of picking "good" and "bad" fruits, focus on these three things:

1. **Choose whole fruit over juice.** Whole fruit keeps its fibre. Juice loses most of it.
2. **Watch the portion.** Diabetes UK says it's easy to overdo it if you eat a whole bowl. A portion is roughly what fits in the palm of your hand.
3. **Spread it out.** Have your fruit across the day rather than several portions in one sitting.

## How big is a portion of fruit?

The NHS says one adult portion of fruit is 80g of fresh, tinned or frozen fruit, or 30g of dried fruit. Here are some everyday examples from the NHS and Diabetes UK.

| Type of fruit | One portion looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Small fruit | 2 plums, 2 satsumas, 2 kiwi fruit, 3 apricots, 7 strawberries or 14 cherries |
| Medium fruit | 1 apple, banana, pear, orange or nectarine |
| Large fruit | Half a grapefruit, 1 slice of melon (5cm), 1 large slice of pineapple or 2 slices of mango (5cm) |
| Grapes | 1 handful |
| Tinned fruit | 2 pear or peach halves, or 2 pineapple rings in natural juice |
| Frozen fruit | 2 handfuls (4 heaped tablespoons) of frozen blueberries |
| Dried fruit | 1 heaped tablespoon of raisins or sultanas, 2 figs, 3 prunes or 2 Medjool dates |

Diabetes UK says these portion sizes are a guide based on an average healthy adult. Your dietitian can advise you on portions that are right for you.

## What about dried fruit?

Dried fruit counts towards your 5 A Day, but the portion is small. Diabetes UK says a portion is just one tablespoon, so it's easy to eat several without noticing.

The NHS adds that dried fruit can be high in sugar and can be bad for your teeth. It recommends eating dried fruit at mealtimes, for example as part of a dessert, rather than as a snack between meals.

## Can diabetics drink fruit juice and smoothies?

You can, but keep it small. The NHS says to limit fruit juice and smoothies to a combined total of 150ml a day, which is one small glass, and Diabetes UK gives the same 150ml limit.

There are a few reasons for this:

- **It counts as free sugar.** Diabetes UK says the sugar in fruit juice and smoothies counts towards your free sugar intake, unlike whole fruit.
- **There's less fibre.** Juice doesn't contain as much fibre as the fruit itself, and Diabetes UK says it's easy to drink too much in a short time, which can raise blood glucose levels.
- **It only counts once.** The NHS says unsweetened juice and smoothies can only ever count as one portion of your 5 A Day, however much you drink.

Watch out for drinks labelled "juice drink". The NHS says these are unlikely to count towards your 5 A Day, and Diabetes UK warns that some smoothies and juice drinks have extra sugar added, so read the ingredients.

## Is tinned and frozen fruit OK?

Yes. Both count towards your 5 A Day, and frozen fruit is handy for porridge or yoghurt.

With tinned fruit, the NHS and Diabetes UK both say to choose fruit tinned in natural juice rather than syrup. Always check the label to be sure there's no added sugar.

## Simple ways to fit fruit into your day

- Add a handful of berries to porridge or plain yoghurt at breakfast.
- Have an apple, pear or two satsumas as a snack instead of biscuits. Our [best snacks for type 2 diabetes](/best-snacks-for-type-2-diabetes/) has more ideas.
- Keep frozen fruit in the freezer so you always have some in.
- Choose a whole orange over a glass of orange juice.
- Keep dried fruit for mealtimes, such as a spoonful on cereal.

If you take medication for diabetes or have been given a specific eating plan, talk to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian before making big changes to your diet.

## Checking meals without a label

Fruit is easy to spot, but the sugar in a fruit salad, smoothie or dessert when you're eating out is harder to judge. The Dietary Insight app can estimate the sugar in a meal from a photo and suggest a swap, which can help you see where most of the sugar on your plate comes from.
