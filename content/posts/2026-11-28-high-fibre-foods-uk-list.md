**What are the best high fibre foods in the UK?** Beans, lentils, peas, porridge oats, wholemeal bread, wholewheat pasta, potatoes with their skins on, nuts, seeds, fruit and vegetables are all good sources. UK guidelines say adults should aim for 30g of fibre a day, but the NHS says most of us only manage about 20g.

## How much fibre do you need each day?

The NHS says government guidelines recommend adults eat 30g of fibre a day as part of a healthy balanced diet. Children need less, but the NHS says they still need more than they currently get.

| Age | Fibre a day |
|---|---|
| 2 to 5 years | about 15g |
| 5 to 11 years | about 20g |
| 11 to 16 years | about 25g |
| Adults | 30g |

The NHS says children aged 11 to 18 get 16g a day on average. Estimates for adults vary a little by source: the NHS says about 20g, Diabetes UK around 19g and the British Heart Foundation (BHF) around 17g. Whichever figure you use, most of us are well short of 30g.

## Why is fibre good for you?

The NHS says eating plenty of fibre is associated with a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer. It says choosing foods with fibre can also help with your digestion.

The BHF says some soluble fibre, such as the oat beta glucans in porridge, can help lower blood cholesterol by reducing how much cholesterol is absorbed in your gut. It also says high-fibre foods leave you feeling fuller and tend to contain fewer calories.

## Which UK foods are high in fibre?

The BHF gives these approximate figures per 100g. Fibre content depends on how a food is made or prepared, and on how much of it you eat, so treat them as a guide.

| Food | Fibre per 100g (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Red kidney beans | 11.1g |
| Wholemeal bread, toasted | 8.2g |
| Porridge oats | 7.8g |
| Peanuts, dry roasted | 7.6g |
| Green and brown lentils, boiled | 7.4g |
| Peas, boiled | 5.6g |
| Sweet potato, baked | 5.2g |
| Baked beans in tomato sauce | 4.9g |
| Wholewheat spaghetti | 4.2g |
| Broccoli, steamed | 3.8g |
| Raspberries | 3.7g |

Beans and lentils stand out, and they're usually cheap. Tinned versions work well in stews, curries and salads.

## What does a 30g day look like?

The NHS gives some example meals:

1. **Breakfast:** 2 slices of wholemeal toast with peanut butter, then a medium orange, gives around 8.3g.
2. **Lunch:** wholemeal spaghetti with a lentil and tomato sauce gives around 11g.
3. **Dinner:** grilled chicken breast with a skin-on baked potato, carrots and green beans gives around 11.2g.

Together that comes to about 30g, without any special products.

## What are easy swaps to eat more fibre?

These tips come from the NHS and Diabetes UK:

- **Breakfast:** choose a higher-fibre cereal such as plain wholewheat biscuits, plain shredded wholegrain or porridge.
- **Bread:** go for wholemeal, granary or seeded bread. Diabetes UK notes that brown bread is not as high in fibre as wholemeal. Our guide to the [best bread for diabetics](/best-bread-for-diabetics-uk/) has more on this.
- **Pasta and rice:** swap white for wholewheat pasta, brown rice or bulgur wheat. Diabetes UK also mentions wholewheat couscous and quinoa.
- **Potatoes:** keep the skins on, for example a baked potato or boiled new potatoes.
- **Pulses:** add beans, lentils or chickpeas to stews, curries and salads.
- **Snacks:** try fresh fruit, vegetable sticks, rye crackers, oatcakes, or unsalted nuts or seeds with no added sugar.

The NHS says dried fruit is sticky and can increase the risk of tooth decay, so it's better eaten as part of a meal than as a between-meal snack.

## How do you spot high fibre foods on a label?

The BHF says foods labelled "high fibre" must contain 6g of fibre per 100g, and foods labelled "source of fibre" must contain 3g per 100g.

Diabetes UK suggests comparing the nutrition labels on the back of packs and choosing the one with more fibre. This works well for cereals, bread and crackers.

## Is fibre helpful if you have diabetes?

Diabetes UK says higher-fibre foods have less of an effect on blood glucose levels. The BHF says eating more fibre, especially cereal fibre and wholegrains, can help manage blood sugar levels. Our [low GI foods list](/low-gi-foods-list-uk/) covers many of the same foods.

Diabetes UK says aiming for 30g a day may feel like a lot, and increasing slowly towards it is fine. Always speak to your GP, diabetes nurse or dietitian before making big changes to your diet.

## Are there any downsides to eating more fibre?

The BHF says to increase fibre gradually and drink plenty of water to help your gut adjust. Diabetes UK suggests water, sugar-free drinks, or unsweetened tea or coffee with milk if you have diabetes.

The BHF says relying too much on fibre powders can cause bloating and digestive discomfort, and can reduce how well you absorb minerals like iron and calcium. People with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), Crohn's disease or other gut conditions may need individual advice, so check with your GP or a dietitian.

Home-cooked and restaurant meals don't come with a label. The Dietary Insight app can estimate what's on your plate from a photo and suggest a swap, such as wholegrain instead of white. It's an estimate, so use it alongside your healthcare team's advice.
