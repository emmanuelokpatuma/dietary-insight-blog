**How are saturated fat and cholesterol foods linked?** The NHS says eating too much saturated fat can raise "bad" LDL cholesterol in your blood, which can increase the risk of heart disease and stroke. Foods high in saturated fat include fatty meat, butter, ghee, cheese, cream, pastries and coconut oil. Swapping some of them for unsaturated fats, such as olive or rapeseed oil, can help.

## What does saturated fat do to cholesterol?

Cholesterol is a fatty substance in your blood. The British Heart Foundation (BHF) says eating too much saturated fat can raise blood cholesterol, in particular the "bad" kind, known as non-HDL cholesterol. A high level increases your risk of heart attack and stroke.

The NHS says there's good evidence that replacing saturated fats with some unsaturated fats can help lower your cholesterol level. So the aim isn't to cut out fat altogether, but to change the type of fat you eat most often.

## How much saturated fat a day?

The NHS gives these daily maximums:

| Who | Saturated fat limit |
|---|---|
| Men | No more than 30g a day |
| Women | No more than 20g a day |
| Children | Less than adults |

The BHF says this works out at less than 10% of your calories. It adds that people who have heart disease or are at high risk may need to eat less than this. The NHS says most people in the UK eat too much saturated fat.

## Which foods are high in saturated fat?

The NHS says most saturated fat comes from animal sources, such as meat and dairy, as well as some plant foods like palm oil and coconut oil. Foods high in it include:

1. Fatty cuts of meat, sausages, bacon and pies
2. Cured meats like salami, chorizo and pancetta
3. Butter, ghee, lard and suet
4. Cheese, especially hard cheese like cheddar
5. Cream, soured cream, crème fraîche and ice cream
6. Biscuits, cakes and pastries, such as croissants and sausage rolls
7. Chocolate and chocolate spreads
8. Coconut oil, coconut milk, coconut cream and palm oil

To give a sense of scale, the BHF estimates a matchbox-size piece of cheddar (30g) has about 6g of saturated fat, a tablespoon of butter about 8g, and one sausage roll about 13g.

## Which foods add the most saturated fat to UK diets?

The BHF, using national survey data, says just nine foods are responsible for most of the saturated fat we eat. The biggest sources are:

| Food group | Share of saturated fat |
|---|---|
| Red and processed meats | 15% |
| Cakes, biscuits, buns and pastries | 10% |
| Cheese | 9% |
| Pizza, pasta and rice dishes | 6% |
| Butter | 6% |
| Milk | 6% |
| Chicken, turkey and dishes made with them | 6% |
| Chocolate | 5% |
| Egg dishes | 5% |

This is useful because it shows where small changes add up. Swapping one or two everyday foods may make more difference than avoiding an occasional treat.

## Do eggs and shellfish raise cholesterol?

Not much, for most people. The BHF says dietary cholesterol does not have a big impact on blood cholesterol for most people, and lists eggs, shellfish and lean meat as foods you can still enjoy.

How you cook them matters more. The BHF says egg dishes made with butter, cheese or cream contribute much more saturated fat than a plain boiled or poached egg. If you have familial hypercholesterolaemia, the BHF says you may need to limit dietary cholesterol, so ask your GP or dietitian.

## Is coconut oil a healthy choice?

The BHF says coconut oil is often reported as healthy, but there's no scientific evidence to support this. It's about 86% saturated fat, more than butter. Palm oil is nearly 50% saturated fat.

The BHF suggests using coconut and palm oils sparingly, as you would butter, and choosing olive, rapeseed or sunflower oil for everyday cooking. This is worth knowing if your cooking uses coconut milk or palm oil, which the NHS also lists as high in saturated fat.

## What are healthier fats to choose instead?

The NHS lists these sources of unsaturated fat:

1. Olive oil, rapeseed oil and spreads made from them
2. Avocados
3. Nuts such as almonds, walnuts and peanuts
4. Oily fish, such as salmon, mackerel, sardines and trout

The NHS recommends at least 2 portions of fish a week, with 1 being an oily fish. Our guide to [foods that can help lower blood pressure](/foods-that-lower-blood-pressure/) has more on building heart-friendly meals.

## How can I read the label for saturated fat?

The NHS uses these per 100g guides, which match the traffic-light colours on front-of-pack labels:

| Saturates per 100g | Rating |
|---|---|
| More than 5g | High (red) |
| 1.5g to 5g | Medium (amber) |
| 1.5g or less | Low (green) |

The NHS suggests choosing products with green or amber for saturated fat. Be careful with "lower fat" claims: the NHS says these must have at least 30% less fat than a similar product, but can still be high in fat. Our guide on [how to read food labels](/how-to-read-food-labels-diabetes/) explains the rest of the label.

## What simple swaps can I make?

These ideas come from the NHS and BHF:

1. Use lower-fat mince, or brown mince and drain off the fat.
2. Choose back bacon instead of streaky, and grill it rather than fry.
3. Try a tomato-based pasta sauce instead of a creamy or cheesy one.
4. Use a strong-tasting cheese, such as reduced-fat mature cheddar, so you need less.
5. Switch to 1% or skimmed milk. The NHS says skimmed has about half the saturated fat of semi-skimmed.
6. Take the skin off chicken, and grill, bake, poach or steam rather than fry.
7. When eating out, choose dry or tomato-based curries like tandoori, or a shish kebab instead of a doner.

If you have high cholesterol or take medicine for it, talk to your GP, practice nurse or dietitian about the right changes for you.
