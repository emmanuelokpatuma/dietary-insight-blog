Alcohol and Type 2 Diabetes: UK Units, Hypo Risk and Tips

Can you drink alcohol with type 2 diabetes? UK unit limits, how alcohol can cause a hypo, when to call 999 and simple tips for drinking more safely.

Can you drink alcohol if you have type 2 diabetes? The NHS alcohol guidance applies to everyone, but with diabetes there’s one extra thing to plan for. The NHS lists drinking a lot of alcohol, particularly without eating, as a cause of low blood sugar, and it advises everyone to stay within 14 units a week. If you take diabetes medicines, ask your GP or diabetes nurse how alcohol fits with your treatment.

How does alcohol affect blood sugar?

The NHS says low blood sugar (a hypo) is usually when your blood glucose is below 4mmol/L. One of the causes it lists is drinking a lot of alcohol, particularly without eating.

The NHS also explains that diabetes medicines lower your blood sugar, but can sometimes make it go too low. This is common with insulin, and can also happen with some other diabetes medicines, such as gliclazide and glimepiride.

Your risk depends on your treatment, so your GP, diabetes nurse or pharmacist is the best person to ask.

How much alcohol is low risk?

The NHS advises men and women not to drink more than 14 units a week on a regular basis. If you regularly drink that much, spread it over 3 or more days and try to have several drink-free days each week.

The NHS calls this “low risk” rather than “safe”, because there’s no safe drinking level. It defines binge drinking as more than 8 units in a single session for men, or more than 6 units for women.

How many units are in common drinks?

One unit is 10ml or 8g of pure alcohol. The NHS says that’s around the amount the average adult can process in an hour. Here are its figures for common drinks:

So 14 units is about 6 pints of average-strength beer or 10 small glasses of lower-strength wine. A couple of large glasses of wine can use up almost half of your weekly guideline.

You can work out units yourself: strength (ABV) x volume (ml) ÷ 1,000. A pint of 5.2% lager is 5.2 x 568 ÷ 1,000, which is just under 3 units.

How can you drink more safely with diabetes?

Based on the NHS advice, a few practical steps:

  1. Don’t drink on an empty stomach. The NHS hypo page says do not drink alcohol without eating. Waiting for your evening meal before you have a drink is one of the NHS Better Health tips.
  2. Choose lower-strength drinks. NHS Better Health suggests trying lighter beers under 4% ABV and other lower-strength drinks.
  3. Write off the rounds. This is another NHS Better Health tip for drinking less.
  4. Keep some days drink-free. This is part of the NHS low-risk guidance.
  5. Watch the calories. NHS Better Health says alcoholic drinks are high in calories. Our guide to reading food labels with diabetes can help you spot sugar in mixers and soft drinks.
  6. Know the signs of a hypo. The NHS lists feeling hungry, sweating, shaking, dizziness, feeling anxious or irritable, blurred vision and confusion.

If you drive and are at risk of hypos, the NHS says to check your blood sugar before each journey and every 2 hours while driving.

What should you do if you have a hypo after drinking?

The NHS says to treat a hypo with something sugary and quick-acting, such as a small glass of fruit juice or a sugary fizzy drink, then check your blood sugar after 10 to 15 minutes. Once it’s back above 4mmol/L, eat something longer-acting. Your diabetes team can tell you exactly how to treat hypos with your medicines.

Call 999 if you think someone has very low blood sugar and they’re not responding normally or they’re unconscious, and they’ve been drinking alcohol. The NHS also says to call 999 if a glucagon injection isn’t available or you don’t know how to use it, or if they haven’t recovered within 10 minutes of a glucagon injection. Don’t give an unconscious person food or drink, as they won’t be able to swallow safely.

See a GP if you have diabetes and you get hypos often, you’ve had severe hypos or you’ve had hypos at night. You can also speak to your diabetes care team.

Does alcohol affect blood pressure too?

If you’re also watching your blood pressure, NHS Better Health says that if you drink nearly every day, you might be more at risk of high blood pressure. Our guide to foods that can help lower blood pressure has more on eating well for your heart.

Where can you get help to cut down?

If you’d like support, NHS Better Health says you can call Drinkline free on 0300 123 1110. If you drink heavily and get physical withdrawal symptoms, the NHS says to get medical advice before stopping, because it can be dangerous to stop too quickly without proper help.

Frequently asked questions

Can alcohol cause a hypo if you have type 2 diabetes?

It can. The NHS lists drinking a lot of alcohol, particularly without eating, as a cause of low blood sugar, and says some diabetes medicines can make blood sugar go too low. Ask your GP or diabetes nurse whether your treatment puts you at risk.

How many units of alcohol a week is low risk?

The NHS advises men and women not to drink more than 14 units a week on a regular basis, spread over 3 or more days, with several drink-free days each week. It calls this low risk rather than safe, because there's no safe drinking level.

How many units are in a glass of wine?

The NHS says a standard 175ml glass of 12% wine is 2.1 units, a small 125ml glass is 1.5 units and a large 250ml glass is 3 units. A 750ml bottle of 13.5% wine contains 10 units.
